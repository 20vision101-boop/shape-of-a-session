01 · THE PREMISE

Part One — Muscle is the biggest glucose sink you own

Skeletal muscle takes up the large majority of the glucose your body clears after a meal, and it has a second door for doing it. Insulin opens one. Contraction opens the other — the same GLUT4 transporters move to the cell surface when a muscle works, whether or not insulin shows up.

That's why a ten-minute walk bends a post-meal curve, and why resistance training pays out over months: a session empties muscle glycogen and leaves it hungry, while more lean mass means a bigger tank to fill next time. One is the acute effect, the other is the adaptation. You want both.

What follows is the practical half — the zones worth training in, the four schools most of this advice descends from, a library of movements with step-by-step instructions, four weekly templates that hold them, and a place to keep your own numbers.

02 · THE ZONES

Part Two — Not all effort is the same effort

Intensity is usually described as a percentage of maximum heart rate. The bands below are the common five-zone model, with the fuel mix and the reason you'd train in each. Rough estimate for your max: 208 − 0.7 × age — a lab test is better, but this is close enough to aim with.

Most weekly volume belongs low (Zone 2 conversational work), a small slice belongs high (Zone 4-5 intervals), and the middle is where people accidentally live — hard enough to accumulate fatigue, easy enough to skip the adaptation either end would have given them.

03 · THE SCHOOLS

Part Three — Four traditions, four answers

Nobody trains from first principles; everybody inherits a school. The four below disagree productively — about how much variety a program needs, about whether intensity or practice drives adaptation, about what tissue you're even training. Each card ends with the movements that school actually programs, and with an honest note on where its claims outrun its evidence.

They overlap more than their internet arguments suggest. A hinge is a hinge whether the load is a barbell or a bell; the disagreement is about dose, variety, and what gets measured. The fourth is the one where the gap between anatomy and marketing is widest — its card and its movements are written to keep those apart.

05 · THE WEEK

Part Five — Four weeks, one per school

The same seven days, arranged four ways. Pick a template to see how each tradition spends a week, then click a day for the session it's asking for.

How any of them progresses

  1. Add reps before weight. Work the top of the rep range on every set, then add the smallest load available and drop back to the bottom of the range.
  2. Leave 1-3 reps in reserve. Stop a set when you could still do a couple more clean ones. Grinding to failure on every set buys fatigue, not adaptation.
  3. Hold the easy days easy. Zone 2 that creeps into Zone 3 is the most common way to make a week harder without making it better.
  4. Deload every 4-6 weeks. Same movements, ~60% of the usual sets, for one week. It's part of the plan, not a failure of it.
